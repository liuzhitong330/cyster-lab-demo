The Cyster Lab studies how immune cells find the right tissue compartment and how those spatial decisions shape an immune response. A recent study asks what happens at the lymph-node gateway when inflammation suppresses the homeostatic CCL21 cue around high endothelial venules: what signal takes over, and which cells build it?

I built a small interactive reading of that switch, so you can compare wild-type and EBI2-deficient B-cell entry across homeostatic, inflamed, and tumor settings, then trace how neighboring cell types assemble the replacement oxysterol gradient.

How it works

The first view organizes the competitive homing and spatial-imaging results reported by Chen and colleagues. Choose a tissue context and transferred B-cell genotype to see which cue is available and whether the cell moves beyond the venular niche. The second view follows the transcellular pathway from cholesterol to 7α,25-HC and identifies the contribution of HEV endothelium, fibroblastic reticular cells, Langerhans cells, and EBI2+ B cells.

This is a structured interpretation of reported results and public GSE281684 metadata, not a raw reanalysis of the approximately 1.7 GB ATAC-seq files.

In a homeostatic node, CCL21 around the venule supports the familiar CCR7-guided route. After local viral inflammation, CCL21 falls while CCL19 is retained, and EBI2 becomes much more important for B-cell recruitment. At 90 minutes, EBI2-deficient B cells were 50–70% less represented in inflamed draining nodes; wild-type B cells also moved roughly twice as far from the venule as their EBI2-deficient counterparts.

Endothelial Ch25h produces 25-HC at the gateway; fibroblastic reticular-cell Cyp7b1 completes the ligand; and EBI2 lets an arriving B cell interpret the resulting cue. Langerhans cells add another Ch25h source after skin inflammation. GSE281684 complements this mechanism with four ATAC-seq samples from sorted CD45− CD31− gp38+ reticular cells, comparing inflamed popliteal and non-draining brachial nodes 24 hours after footpad LCMV.

Why this matters for the lab

The result turns lymphocyte entry into a tissue-level computation: one cell makes an intermediate, another completes the ligand, and the arriving lymphocyte must express the matching receptor. A useful next experiment would pair short-term competitive homing with flow-sorted stromal populations and spatial imaging across a time course, connecting receptor dependence to when and where the tissue builds the signal.
